# Dermoscopy-Based AI Risk Scoring Enhanced Experienced Dermatologists’ Decision-Making: a Large Retrospective Reader Study

## Abstract

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in dermoscopy is promising for diagnostic and management decisions.

This study aimed to assess the impact of a deep learning-generated AI risk score, based on dermoscopic images and metadata, on dermatologists’ diagnostic accuracy, confidence, and management strategy for lesions suspicious of skin cancer.

A neural network was developed using a database of dermoscopic images of benign and malignant skin lesions, including both proprietary and public data. In a multicenter, cross-sectional study, 104 experienced dermatologists, with a median dermoscopic experience of 10 years, evaluated batches from a test set of 922 skin lesions, including 577 benign (63%) and 345 malignant (37%) cases, resulting in a dataset comprising 9,198 observations. Each case was assessed before and after the AI-generated risk score. Key outcomes included accurate diagnosis, correct decision on malignancy, and management strategy.

Overall accuracy for correct diagnosis increased from 74.6% before AI to 81.6% after AI (95% confidence interval (CI): 3.8%–10.2%; P<0.001). Overall sensitivity for decision on malignancy increased from 91.7% before AI, to 97.6% after AI (95% CI: 2.9%–8.8%; P<0.001). The biggest increase in sensitivity was seen for melanoma and high-grade dysplastic naevi, from 79.0% before AI to 93.8% after AI (95% CI: 5.4%–24.4%; P=0.002). AI was significantly associated with choosing the correct management strategy (53.5% to 59.0%, 95% CI: 2.5%–8.5%; P<0.001).

AI enhanced experienced dermatologists’ decision-making, particularly for melanoma and high-grade dysplastic naevi. Future prospective studies should further explore AI’s integration into routine clinical settings.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Dermoscopy, Skin neoplasms, Melanoma, Diagnostic accuracy

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a rapidly developing technology in healthcare, particularly in dermatology. Given its exponential advancement, AI is likely to transform the diagnostic landscape of dermatology. Several studies have evaluated an AI risk score based on dermoscopic images for the assessment of skin cancer. When comparing AI to human dermatologist performance [–], AI did equal or better than humans. However, to implement AI technology safely and effectively into routine clinical care, especially as a tool for clinical decision-making, a different approach is needed. Rather than testing AI as a stand-alone device, we need to study the impact of AI on the clinical decision of a dermatologist, advocating for a complementary rather than a comparative approach, for which the term Augmented Intelligence (AuI) is often used. Research with regards to complementary approaches [] has partly focused on readers, many of whom have limited experience in dermoscopy and are therefore likely to benefit more from AI assistance.

## Objectives

This study assessed the impact of a deep learning AI risk score on diagnostic accuracy and management in experienced dermatologists. In a realistic setting with patient metadata and images, we aimed to show how AI may enhance decision-making and improve outcomes.

## Methods

The methodology employed in this study was designed to develop and evaluate the impact of our neural network model. Firstly, we trained our own network, which required the collection and preparation of a suitable training and test set. Once both the neural network and the test set were established, we conducted a reader study. Each aspect of this methodology is described in the subsequent sections.

## Neural Network

For the development of the AI algorithm, a database of dermoscopic images with matching metadata was established, consisting of both own data (Dermscan database) and external data (HAM10000 [] and BCN20000 [] database). The dermoscopic images of the Dermscan database were collected at the university hospitals of Leuven and Gent as part of the multicenter Dermscan project (2016–2023, trial approved by Ethics Committee of Leuven s60193). All patients gave informed consent for the publication of their case details. A diagnosis was linked to the image, based on expert opinion from clinicians who had seen the patient in person or through histopathology, when available. The Dermscan database includes 10,138 images from 6,977 lesions across 2,719 patients.

The overall architecture of the neural network was based on the public state of the art in AI for dermatology. At the ISIC challenge 2018 [], a challenge to develop the best AI on a public dermoscopy dataset, it became clear that a number of concepts returned with most top competitors [–]. To optimize the lesion risk scoring performance, three state-of-the-art strategies were combined: ensembling [,], incorporation of metadata [–], and test time augmentation [,]. Because images from the Dermscan database were taken with a wider field of view than those of the public datasets, a second AI was applied that first detects the region of interest to ensure that the AI focused on the actual lesion [].

## Test Set

A test set of 922 lesions was selected from the Dermscan database, in line with seven diagnostic categories () after application of exclusion criteria (). Of the total cases, 345 (37%) were classified as malignant, comprising basal cell carcinoma (BCC, N=117; 13%), melanoma and high-grade dysplastic naevus (MEL–HGDN, N=120; 13%), and actinic keratosis, Bowen’s disease, and squamous cell carcinoma (AK–BD–SCC, N=108; 12%). The remaining 577 cases (63%) were benign, including naevi (NV, N=476; 52%), benign keratinocytic lesions such as solar lentigo and seborrheic keratosis (BKL, N=81; 9%), dermatofibroma (DF, N=9; 1%), and vascular lesions (VASC, N=11; 1%). AK were classified in the malignant category, in line with the approach by Tschandl et al. []. HGDN were likewise included among the malignant cases, as their biological potential and clinical management, namely the recommendation for complete excision, distinguishes them from benign lesions. All lesions of MEL-HGDN, BCC, SCC, and BD types had a pathology-proven diagnosis. The other lesion types were a mix of both pathology and expert consensus. The lesion distribution for the benign cases was based on the lesion distribution presented by Tschandl et al. [], where the ratio of malignant-to-benign lesions is slightly below 4:10. Test set size and lesion distribution were defined to provide adequate statistical power (>0.8).

## Study Design

We used Belgian dermatology congresses and local accreditation groups to recruit dermatologists to participate. To enroll in the study, participants anonymously had to fill out a questionnaire asking for details on their sex, age (continuous), work place, hours of training in dermoscopy (1–5 hours, 5–10 hours, or >10 hours), and years of experience in dermoscopy (continuous). They were asked for their license number to ensure they enrolled only once. Their baseline demographic characteristics are presented in . We recruited 104 experienced dermatologists, with a median dermoscopic experience of 10 years. Each reader was asked to evaluate 50 unique cases, but they could also handle fewer or additional cases. Each of the 104 dermatologists completed 50 cases.

The batches of 50 cases were randomly selected from the test set and consisted, in line with the test set, of 32 benign cases and 18 malignant cases, six in each malignant lesion group (MEL-HGDN, AK-BD-SCC, and BCC). The reader was given information on the composition of the malignant groups but not on the distribution of malignancy.

For each case, readers were shown dermoscopic and clinical images along with patient age, sex, and lesion location. In Step 1, they provided a diagnosis, confidence score, and management strategy. After viewing the AI risk score (0–100%; <40% benign, >60% malignant, 40–60% inconclusive), readers could modify their responses in Step 2 (). The final database was a retrospective dataset containing 9,198 observations, where the gold standard (true diagnosis) was known through histopathology or expert consensus.

## Outcomes of Interest

There were four outcomes of interest in this study: correct decision on malignancy (Yes/No), correct diagnosis (Yes/No), confidence in diagnosis decision (0–100%), and correct management strategy (Ideal/Possible/Incorrect).

For each case, the reader could indicate his/her decision on management strategy from the following options: no action, biopsy, curettage, excision, or treatment: topical treatment (5-fluorouracil (5-FU) or imiquimod), cryotherapy, photodynamic treatment (PDT), CO2 laser, or other (free text). The management strategy responses were grouped into three categories for further statistical analysis: “Ideal”, “Possible”, and “Incorrect”. For the four benign diagnoses, the ideal strategy was “no action”; for the three malignant diagnoses, the ideal and possible strategies are defined in . BCC was subtyped in some cases (e.g., superficial, nodular, mixed, or infiltrating), and specific management strategies were defined as ideal for these subtypes. When the BCC subtype was not specified via histopathology, different management options were considered correct. A biopsy was always considered a possible option, except in the case of benign lesions and melanoma.

## Software

All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software []. Mixed models were fitted in R using the lme4 package [].

## Statistical Methodology

For all outcomes of interest, a mixed model was used with a random effect for the different cases and another random effect for the dermatologists, nested within the random effect of the cases. Therefore, the random dermatologist effect was not unique to each dermatologist; instead, it uniquely defined the different dermatologists within a case. To account for variability among dermatologists, several fixed effects were added to the model: age, sex, hours of dermoscopy training, and years of experience in dermoscopy. Additionally, the model included a binary variable for the impact of AI (with two levels: “before AI” and “after AI,” indicating whether the observation was recorded prior to or after the dermatologist was presented with the AI score), a variable for the AI risk score (measured as the absolute deviation from a score of 50%), and a variable for the true diagnosis. The absolute deviation from a 50% risk score was included to reflect the expected differential impact of more extreme scores (e.g., 100%) compared to scores closer to 50% (e.g., 61%), even though both signify malignancy.

## Results

The frequency of correct malignancy decision before and after AI intervention for all answers (N=4,599) is compared in . Overall, correct malignancy decisions increased in all lesion categories besides dermatofibroma, where 97.6% of cases were evaluated as benign both before and after AI risk score was shown. The biggest differences in improvement were seen in the three malignant categories.

Overall, AI intervention had a significant positive effect on the proportion of correct malignancy decision, with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.67 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.28–5.57; P=0.009). Overall sensitivity and specificity for decision on malignancy went from respectively 91.7% (95% CI: 87.6%–95.8%) and 84.2% (95% CI: 79.1%–89.3%) before AI, to 97.6% (95% CI: 95.9%–99.2%) and 86.7% (CI: 82.2%–91.3%) after AI. Sensitivity significantly increased by 5.9% (95% CI: 2.9%–8.8%; P<0.001), while the rise in specificity (2.5%; 95% CI: 0.0%–5.0%; P=0.054) was not statistically significant.

The increase in sensitivity was mostly seen in the MELHGDN category, with an increase in sensitivity from 79.0% to 93.8% (difference of 14.9%, 95% CI 5.4%–24.4%; P=0.002), while sensitivity in the BCC category increased from 96.9% to 98.8% (difference of 1.9%, 95% CI: 0.2%– 3.9%; P=0.072), and sensitivity in the AK-BD-SCC category increased from 96.5% to 99.2% (difference of 2.7%, 95% CI: 0%–5.3%; P=0.046). As the effect of risk score was significant, the impact of AI on the sensitivity was assessed for each diagnosis at different risk score values. Comparing the different malignant classes showed that the impact that the AI risk score had on sensitivity was greater when the risk score was closer to 60% than to 100% and was most pronounced in the MEL-HGDN category (see ).

For correct diagnosis, improvements were seen across all diagnostic categories besides DF (see ). AI intervention significantly improved the odds of correct diagnosis (OR=1.82: 95% CI: 1.07–3.07; P=0.026). Overall accuracy for correct diagnosis went from 74.6% (95% CI: 68.3%– 80.9%) to 81.6% (95% CI: 76.4%–86.8%; difference of 7.0%, 95% CI: 3.8%–10.2%; P<0.001).

Regarding confidence in diagnosis, the average levels across all malignant and benign lesion categories () increased after AI assistance. On average, confidence increased by 2% (95% CI: 1.8%– 2.7%), with a median change of 0% (IQR: −3%–10%), although this increase was not statistically significant.

When comparing management strategies (incorrect, possible, ideal) before and after AI (), the percentage of ideal strategies increased slightly, from 44% to 45% post-AI, while incorrect strategies decreased from 49% to 48%. After dichotomizing the outcome, AI intervention was significantly associated with choosing ideal management strategies (OR=1.36; 95% CI: 1.16–1.61; P<0.001). Accuracy for correct management strategy increased significantly from 53.5% (95% CI: 48%–59.1%) to 59.0% (95% CI: 53.5%– 64.6%; difference of 5.5% (95% CI: 2.5%–8.5%); P<0.001).

## Conclusions

In this study, AI intervention, more specifically a deep learning-generated AI risk score based on dermoscopic images and metadata, improved dermatologists’ diagnostic accuracy and correct management strategies, especially for the three categories of malignancy: MEL-HGDN, AK-BD-SCC, and BCC. The biggest increase in sensitivity for correct decision on malignancy was seen in the MEL-HGDN category, which is the most difficult diagnostic group in real life.

Several studies have been published discussing and evaluating AI for the assessment of skin cancer. Combining AI with human expertise improves diagnostic accuracy, as shown by Hekler et al. [], who found an accuracy of 82.95% for the combination, compared to 81.59% and 42.94% achieved by, respectively, artificial or human intelligence alone. A retrospective reader study by Tschandl et al. [] demonstrated that using AI-based multiclass probabilities for decision support improved the accuracy of human raters from 63.6% to 77.0%. Similarly, Winkler et al. [] reported in their prospective study that sensitivity and specificity significantly increased when AI was integrated into dermatologists’ decision-making. They both reported that the greatest improvements were observed for the less experienced dermatologists, while those with more experience showed little or no improvement [,]. Additionally, faulty AI can mislead clinicians, including experts [], highlighting the importance of high-quality AI models and the need for careful integration into clinical workflows.

Our study is distinct from earlier research on several levels. First, regarding the dataset that was used, the AI network in the study by Tschandl et al. [] was trained only on the HAM10000 dataset, which, as the authors emphasized, underperformed on out-of-distribution images not represented in the training data []. A meta-study on the ISIC 2019 challenge [] revealed that state-of-the-art classification methods performance decreased over 20% on datasets specifically designed to more accurately reflect clinical realities, when compared to a previous, well-controlled benchmark. In our study, the AI network was trained not only on HAM10000 but additionally on both the BCN20000 database [], considered a more diverse and challenging dataset, and our own Dermscan database. The Dermscan dataset consists of clinical and dermoscopic images collected from tertiary centers, featuring lesions that are often quite challenging, even for benign cases, since a lot of lesions were excised to rule out malignancy. Each case was accompanied by a histologically confirmed diagnosis or expert consensus, based on a real-life evaluation of both the lesion and the patient, considering their medical history and the history of the lesions.

Secondly, in the study by Tschandl et al. [], where the task involved a multiclass classification problem, readers demonstrated a net gain in performance when using AI-based multiclass probabilities but not when using binary risk scoring. In our study, both multiclass classification tasks and malignancy decision tasks were evaluated before and after readers were presented with an AI-based risk score ranging from 0% to 100%. As outlined in the results, readers improved significantly in accuracy for both malignancy decision and classification (correct diagnosis) tasks.

Furthermore, it is important to note the difference in demographics of the readers. Almost half of the readers of the Tschandl [] study were dermatology residents and general practitioners, and it was emphasized that AI assistance was particularly beneficial for less experienced practitioners. The dermatologists that participated in our study were all experienced dermatologists exclusively, with a median of 10 years of experience in dermoscopy. The majority of our readers (67%) were dermatologists working fully or partly in private practice who are representative of clinicians to include in a reader study, as they conduct skin cancer screenings for the majority of the population. In this study, we demonstrate that even experienced dermatologists benefit from AI, as it helps them achieve higher sensitivity by more accurately diagnosing malignant cases, particularly melanoma and high-grade dysplastic naevi.

A systematic review of the literature [] on reader studies highlighted the highly artificial nature of these settings, which rely solely on single, often only dermoscopic, images of cases. This likely leads to an underestimation of dermatologists’ performance in real-life settings. To address this limitation of reader studies, we tried to move beyond testing in highly artificial environments. Instead of exclusively using single images, metadata (age, sex, body part location of the lesion) was incorporated into the AI algorithm with the hope that this would enhance their real-world applicability. To ensure that the impact of AI was then tested in a more realistic setting, in the reader study the dermatologists were presented not with only one dermoscopic image but also with a clinical image and clinical information (age, sex, lesion localization). As outlined in the methodology, AI risk scores, in the range 40–60% are considered inconclusive and are reported as such towards the readers. These cases were not excluded from this study. This approach was taken to assess the implications of these inconclusive results, as would occur in a real-life scenario.

In this study, we focused on an end-to-end solution, where acquisition settings were controlled and the AI was trained specifically for those settings. As a result, out-of-distribution images such as those acquired with different settings or hardware were less of a concern []. However, this approach could introduce a potential risk: the performance of the AI may be lower when applied to other patient populations due to limited generalizability. This is a common issue in many studies [], as datasets predominantly consist of light-skinned skin lesions, reflecting the demographic of patients seen at the participating hospitals, with no targeted recruitment to ensure broader skin tone representation. Additionally, rare tumors (e.g., pyogenic granuloma, warts, sebaceous hyperplasia, dermatofibrosarcoma, Kaposi sarcoma) were not included in our dataset. Their scarcity limits the AI’s ability to perform reliably on these lesions. Another limitation of this study, as with all reader studies, is that clinical assessment of the patient was not possible, making it challenging to identify the “ugly duckling” or signature naevi. Additionally, personal and family history as well as the history of the lesion in question (e.g., new onset, itching, bleeding) was absent. A final limitation is that the lesions included in the datasets (both training and test set) consisted of lesions that were deemed worthy of photography, resulting in a selected population of generally more challenging cases. However, this also reflects the types of cases for which the AI will be utilized rather than clear-cut benign cases. All of the malignant cases in the test set were histologically proven, which provides a solid ground truth but increases the overall difficulty of the lesions. Consequently, the sensitivity of clinicians may be lower than that observed in real-life clinics.

To combat these limitations, future research requires a truly prospective pilot study comparing the clinicians’ diagnoses and management strategies in real-life, face-to-face patient examinations before and after AI risk scoring.

Overall, this research study demonstrates that experienced dermatologists benefited from AI-based risk scoring, which positively impacted decision-making, particularly for malignant lesions such as melanoma and high-grade dysplastic naevi, illustrating its potential to aid in earlier detection of melanoma.

With the help of a deep learning model trained on diverse datasets and clinical metadata, dermatologists improved malignancy detection, lesion classification, and management strategies, ensuring alignment with established best practices. These findings underscore the value of AI as a complementary tool, aiding dermatologists in making more informed and confident decisions.
